01 Propagation in a Lossy Medium

Why we write waves as exponentials, what "attenuation" physically means, and how deep a wave can reach.

Why an exponential and not a sine?

Both cos(kz) and e-jkz describe an oscillation. But the exponential form is preferred because it has a less limited domain: a sinusoid only ever describes an undamped oscillation, whereas the exponential can carry a complex exponent and therefore describe oscillation and decay at the same time. That makes it the natural language for propagation in free space and in a lossy medium, with one single formula.

E(z) = E0 · e−γz   with   γ = α + jβ α = attenuation constant (Np/m) → how fast the amplitude dies. β = phase constant (rad/m) → how fast the phase rotates.

Split it and the physics falls out:

e−γz = e−αz · e−jβz amplitude decay (loss → heat) × pure phase rotation (travel)
Progressive vs regressive wave
  • Progressive wave → e−x → propagation, the transmitted signal travelling from source to ground.
  • Regressive wave → e+x → interaction, the reflected signal travelling from ground back to source.

The direction of propagation is given by the unit vector k̂, which lies on the plane of incidence.

Penetration depth

The single most quotable number of this chapter:

δ = 1 / α the distance at which the field amplitude has fallen to 1/e ≈ 37 % of its surface value

Read it off the decay curve: as α increases, the energy of the EM wave stops earlier — it goes to zero over a shorter distance and is transformed into heat. Conversely, the less attenuation there is, the further the wave reaches into the bottom of the medium = fewer losses.

What defines a medium

A medium is characterised by exactly three constitutive parameters:

ε electrical permittivity — how it stores electric energy / polarises μ magnetic permeability — how it stores magnetic energy σ conductivity — how it turns field into current, i.e. into heat
MediumConductivityConsequence
Conductorσ ≠ 0 (ideally σ → ∞)There are losses: part of the EM energy is converted into heat. α > 0, δ small.
Insulator / perfect dielectricσ = 0No attenuation at all. α = 0, δ → ∞: the wave propagates forever.
Intuition

Conductivity is friction for the electromagnetic field. A metal is "sticky": the field drives currents, the currents dissipate, and the wave dies within microns (that is why radar cannot see inside metal, and why dry sand — nearly a dielectric — can be penetrated for metres by a long-wavelength radar).

Phase velocity tells you the medium

The phase velocity depends on the direction of propagation — so by measuring it you can deduce the type of medium you are in (isotropic vs anisotropic, dense vs rarefied).

vf = ω / β = c / nn = refractive index. Denser medium → larger n → slower wave → shorter wavelength inside.
If they ask

"Why the exponential?" → less limited domain, describes free-space and lossy propagation in one expression, and separates cleanly into decay × phase. "What is δ?" → 1/α, the 1/e depth; large α = shallow penetration = energy turned into heat quickly. "Conductor or dielectric?" → σ ≠ 0 means losses/heat; σ = 0 means perfect dielectric, no attenuation.

02 Polarization

The shape traced by the tip of E — and how we quantify it when the wave is not perfectly behaved.

Definition

Polarization refers to the orientation of the oscillation of the underlying electric field of an EM wave. Formally: it is the locus traced by the tip of the field vector E in the plane orthogonal to the direction of propagation, in the time domain. This locus is always a closed curve and, in the most general case, an ellipse.

The shape of the polarization ellipse depends on only two things:

  1. the amplitudes of the two orthogonal components, Ex and Ey;
  2. their phase difference δ.

The phasor domain

To describe polarization we move to the phasor domain:

E = E0 · e−jβz E0 = the polarization term (a vector: amplitudes + relative phase). e−jβz = the propagation term. Together: a plane wave.
Key remark

It only makes sense to talk about polarization if there is a discontinuity — i.e. two media with different properties. In a single infinite homogeneous medium nothing changes the polarization, so it carries no information.

Fully, partially and un-polarized waves

StateConditionPhysical picture
Completely polarizedOrthogonal components have a deterministic phase and amplitude relationship — a necessary condition for monochromatic wavesThe ellipse is fixed and perfectly defined; it does not change from instant to instant
Partially polarizedRandom fluctuations superimposed: a combination of a fully polarized and an unpolarized componentThe ellipse exists but drifts/wobbles — there is a trend
UnpolarizedNo correlation at all between componentsThe ellipse changes at every instant — no defined shape (natural/thermal light)

The polarization (coherence) matrix W

Because partially polarized waves fluctuate, we cannot describe them with one deterministic ellipse. We use the polarization matrix (a.k.a. coherence matrix), built from the correlations between the orthogonal field components:

W = [ Wxx  Wxy ;  Wyx  Wyy ] = ⟨ E E† ⟩ Diagonal = powers in x and y. Off-diagonal = correlation between the components.
  • tr(W) = Wxx + Wyy = the total power of the wave.
  • The off-diagonal elements represent the correlation between the orthogonal components, normalised by a correlation coefficient µxy.
  • A field is unpolarized if µxy = 0 and Wxx = Wyy (equal power, zero correlation).
  • A field is fully polarized if |µxy| = 1, which implies det(W) = 0.
Why det(W) = 0 means fully polarized

A zero determinant means the matrix has rank 1 — the two components are not independent, one is a fixed complex multiple of the other. That is exactly "a deterministic amplitude and phase relationship", i.e. one single, perfectly defined ellipse.

Degree of polarization P

P = (polarized intensity) / (total intensity),  0 ≤ P ≤ 1 equivalently P = √(1 − 4·det(W)/tr(W)²)
  • P = 1 → the ellipse is perfectly defined (fully polarized).
  • P = 0 → the ellipse is not well defined: too many fluctuations, it varies at every instant (unpolarized).
  • 0 < P < 1 → there is a trend: the ellipse varies gradually (partially polarized).

  • Depolarization: the interaction decreases the degree of polarization P. Typical of volume scattering (many random scatterers each rotating the field a little).

The reason polarization changes at all

It is essential that the boundary conditions on the surface are satisfied — the projections of the incident, reflected and transmitted components must be balanced (tangential E and H continuous). Enforcing this is what forces the polarization to change on interaction, and what gives Fresnel's coefficients their form.

If they ask

"Define polarization" → locus of the tip of E in the plane orthogonal to propagation, in the time domain; always closed; generally an ellipse. "Why a matrix?" → because partially polarized waves fluctuate, so we describe them statistically through correlations. "Unpolarized condition?" → µxy = 0 and Wxx = Wyy. "Fully polarized?" → |µxy| = 1 ⇒ det(W) = 0 ⇒ P = 1.

03 Fresnel Reflection

What a wave can do when it meets an interface, the reflection/transmission coefficients, and the Brewster angle.

RefractionTransmitted through an interfaceThe straw that looks bent in a glass of water

Path loss

The received power Pr is proportional to 1/R². Therefore, as the distance increases there is greater loss. Path loss is defined as

L = Pt / Pr L grows with distance and with frequency. Free-space: L = (4πRf/c)².
Consequence for satellite links

Because loss grows with frequency, the downlink — which uses the lower frequency — suffers fewer losses. That is a deliberate design choice: the satellite has a small power budget, so give the satellite-to-ground direction the easier physics.

Why only certain bands

The frequencies usable for transmission are limited to certain ranges because the atmosphere absorbs certain bands (water vapour ~22 GHz & 183 GHz, oxygen ~60 GHz, plus the IR absorption bands). What is left are the atmospheric windows.

Impedance and the coefficients

η = η0 / n   and   n = c / vf η0 ≈ 377 Ω is free-space impedance; n = refractive index; vf = phase velocity.
Γ = E0r / E0i   (reflection)     τ = E0t / E0i   (transmission) If Γ < 0 there is a phase change (180° inversion) on reflection.

Total reflection = no transmission. The energy that is not reflected is the energy that is transmitted (for a lossless interface: |Γ|² + transmitted fraction = 1).

If they ask

"What happens at Brewster?" → ΓTM = 0: vertical polarization is entirely transmitted, so the reflected wave is purely horizontally polarized. It exists only for TM/vertical; TE never vanishes. "Why is Γ negative for TE?" → phase inversion of 180° on reflection from a denser medium.

04 Apparent Roughness

"Rough" is not a property of the surface alone — it is a property of the surface as seen by a given wavelength and angle.

We need an objective criterion to decide whether a surface is rough or smooth for the incident wave. Consider a wave hitting a point at height 0 m and another at height δ above the mean surface: the two rays travel different paths, so they arrive with a phase difference that depends on the elevation δ.

Since roughness is randomly distributed, the elevation is assumed to be Gaussian distributed, with mean surface height zero and standard deviation σ.

Rayleigh criterion (taking δ ~ σ)
ΔΦ = 4πσ·cos θᵢ / λ Conventionally: ΔΦ < π/2 ⇒ smooth, otherwise rough.

So the apparent roughness depends on the wavelength and the angle of incidence — not on σ alone.

The penalty factor f(σs)

As the surface becomes rougher, energy is scattered more, which decreases the reflected (specular) contribution. This is captured by a penalty factor f(σs) that multiplies — and reduces — the specularly reflected component.

CasePenalty factorPhase differenceBehaviour
Smooth surface (superficie liscia)f(σs) = 1 (at θᵢ = 90°, grazing)ΔΦ = 0Full specular reflection — a mirror. Very little comes back to a monostatic radar.
Rough surface (superficie ruvida)f(σs) = 0ΔΦ ≠ 0Specular component destroyed; energy goes into diffuse scattering in all directions.
Two rules to memorise
  • As the angle of incidence increases (more grazing), less roughness is perceived — cos θᵢ shrinks, ΔΦ shrinks. Look along a rough road at a shallow angle and it glints like a mirror.
  • As the wavelength λ increases, the surface tends to appear smoother. An L-band radar sees a ploughed field as much smoother than an X-band radar does.
If they ask

"Is this field rough?" → never answer without asking "at what λ and what θ?". Quote ΔΦ = 4πσcosθᵢ/λ, the Gaussian σ assumption, and note that roughness kills the specular return and feeds diffuse scattering (which is what a monostatic radar actually measures).

05 Black Body & Thermal Emission

Every object above 0 K glows. Planck tells you at which colour, Wien where the peak is, Stefan–Boltzmann how much in total.

A ray incident on an object generates a reflection and a thermal radiation given by the excitation of the object's electrons.

Black body

A black body absorbs completely without reflecting (it is ideally opaque), and generates thermal radiation proportional to the body's temperature. It is therefore defined as an ideal absorber and ideal emitter.

  • Following Planck's law, the Sun's spectrum peaks in the visible range.
  • The peak of the Earth's radiation is in the infrared (~10 µm).
  • The hotter a star is, the more it emits in the ultraviolet.
  • A nearly perfect black-body spectrum in nature is the cosmic microwave background (T ≈ 2.7 K).

The Earth's energy balance

The Earth receives incident energy from the Sun. Part is diffused or absorbed by the atmosphere; the rest is transmitted to the surface, which absorbs it and transforms it into thermal energy, increasing the temperature. To balance the process, thermal energy is emitted in turn.

Kirchhoff's rule of thumb — memorise this sentence

Reflection and emission are complementary: if a body emits a lot at a certain frequency, then it will reflect little at that frequency, and vice versa. (Formally: emissivity = absorptivity, and for an opaque body absorptivity = 1 − reflectivity.)

Emissivity and brightness temperature

Emissivity e

A dimensionless quantity between 0 and 1 that measures how much a real body emits thermal radiation relative to a black body at the same temperature. It depends on temperature T, polarization p, wavelength λ and direction (θ, φ):

e(θ, φ, p, λ, T) = B / Bbb

The brightness of a real body is then defined from that of a black body, using an equivalent (brightness) temperature TB — the temperature a black body would need to have in order to radiate like the real body:

e(θ, φ, p, λ, T) = TB / T   ⇒   TB = e · T Since e ≤ 1, always TB ≤ T. A radiometer measures TB; getting T requires knowing e.

Gray body

The gray body is a narrow-band approximation of the real body: emissivity is considered constant over certain spectral regions. Therefore its emissivity e does not depend on frequency (within that band). It is the practical working model for most sensors.

The daytime ambiguity

During the day, the reflectance of sunlight and the emissivity of the Earth give comparable contributions in the mid-infrared — so much so that they overlap and become ambiguous. You cannot tell "hot" from "bright" without extra information. This is why thermal work is often done at night, or in bands where one term clearly dominates.

If they ask

"What does a radiometer measure?" → brightness temperature TB = e·T, not physical temperature. "Why is microwave radiometry linear in T?" → Rayleigh–Jeans limit of Planck. "Why does the Earth peak in the IR and the Sun in the visible?" → Wien's displacement law with T ≈ 288 K vs 5778 K.

Observation from the notes

Brightness and radiance are the same thing — only the community differs (microwave people say brightness, optical people say radiance). And power is the integral sum of the powers of each wave.

irradiance ∝ brilliance  →  E = B · π The π (not 2π) comes from integrating cos θ over the hemisphere — the projected-area factor.
The rule about solid angle

The larger the solid angle — and therefore the larger the illuminated area — the lower the power per unit area. Consequently there were more losses. Spreading the same energy over more sky means less of it lands in your pixel.

Also: power is an integral with respect to solid angle AND frequency. Therefore brightness necessarily depends on frequency (it is a spectral density).

Lambertian surface

Definition

A Lambertian surface is one whose spectral radiance is constant with respect to all angles — it looks equally bright from every direction. This is the behaviour of an ideal rough surface.

Note: if the surface were smooth instead, there would be a single direction of reflection, and it would be described by a Dirac delta function.

07 Descriptors

The bookkeeping of energy at a target: what is absorbed, transmitted, scattered — and the radar/optical numbers built from it.

What descriptors are

Descriptors are parameters used to quantify and describe the interactions between electromagnetic radiation and a medium, which can be a surface or a volume. They are fundamental for analysing the energy balance and for understanding radiative transfer processes in Earth observation.

The incident radiation dPi is balanced against three outputs: scattering dPs, transmitted energy dPt, and absorbed energy dPa — which turns into heat (an increase in temperature) and therefore into loss.

Radar cross section (RCS)

σqp = 4πr² · Ssp / Siq p, q = polarization types (transmit / receive). Units: m². The area of an equivalent isotropic scatterer that would return the same power.

NBRCS = Normalized Radar Cross Section in the bistatic case (smooth surface).

Because the target surface is random, we need to average it — the return has both coherent and incoherent components. Normalising by the illuminated area gives a dimensionless scattering coefficient:

σ°qp(Ωi, Ωs) = dσsp / dA "how much radar cross section per square metre of ground" — this is what a SAR image pixel value is.

BRDF and the σ° ↔ ρ bridge

Bidirectional Reflectance Distribution Function links the radar and optical worlds:

σ°qp = 4 ρqp cos θs cos θi
σ scattering coefficient → RADAR ρ reflection coefficient → OPTICAL

Albedo, Lambertian surfaces and Fresnel

The albedo is the integral of the radiation intensity over both polarizations (and all directions). If the surface is Lambertian:

ρ = w i.e. reflectance does not depend on direction or polarization.

By contrast, following Fresnel's theory (a perfectly smooth surface), the reflected power is concentrated in a solid angle defined by 2 Dirac deltas:

Ωs = δ(θs − θi) · δ(φs − φsp) one delta fixes the elevation (angle in = angle out), one fixes the azimuth (specular plane).
And this is why polarization changes

In general, polarization changes on interaction in order to satisfy the boundary conditions of the surface — that is, the projections of the incident, reflected and transmitted ray components must be balanced.

If they ask

"Why normalise the RCS?" → because a distributed target's σ grows with the illuminated area; dividing by A gives a dimensionless, area-independent surface property comparable between sensors. "σ vs ρ?" → same physics, radar vs optical convention, bridged by σ° = 4ρ cosθs cosθi.

Say it like this in the exam

"The brilliance B(r) coming from the origin, B(0), is attenuated by a factor related to the extinction of the material. Two inelastic effects

Raman

Decay of photons to a lower level occurs, accompanied by emission of photons at high frequencies. The scattered photon has a different energy from the incident one — it carries a molecular fingerprint.

Fluorescence

Emission of photons at lower frequencies than the excitation. Used in EO to measure chlorophyll (solar-induced fluorescence → plant photosynthetic activity).

Note

Rayleigh, Mie and optical scattering are elastic (frequency unchanged). Raman and fluorescence are inelastic — the frequency shifts, which is precisely why they carry chemical information.

11 Scatterometer

An active radar that deliberately throws away spatial resolution in exchange for precision — to measure wind over the ocean.

Definition

The scatterometer measures geophysical properties using microwave backscatter from EM pulses. It is an active, non-imaging, real-aperture radar sensor. It essentially measures how much of a signal is returned. It has wide coverage, works day and night and in any weather, and is usually placed on satellites in a near-polar, sun-synchronous orbit.

RCS → NRCS

For a point target, the relationship between incident and scattered wave is the Radar Scattering Cross-Section:

σ = 4πR² · |Es|² / |Ei|²  [m²]

If the target is distributed (the ocean, a forest), the cross-section is normalized, becoming dimensionless:

σ° = (4πR² / A) · ⟨|Es|²⟩ / |Ei|² A = the area illuminated by the incident beam; ⟨·⟩ = ensemble average, because the surface is random. In images it is quoted in dB: σ°(dB) = NRCS.

What controls the return over the sea

The amount of energy returned depends on the roughness of the surface (Rayleigh criterion: the roughness is measured by the difference in phase and path of two incident rays, described statistically by the standard deviation σ). The backscattered energy also depends on the dielectric and geometric properties of the surface — for the sea, on salinity and temperature.

Wave typeScaleRole
Gravitational wavesLong (metres)Carry most of the spectrum's energy; tilt and modulate the short waves
Capillary wavesShort (centimetres)Depend on the local wind — these are the ones the radar resonates with

In the surface roughness spectrum S(k), with k = 2π/λ, the energy is greater in the gravitational-wave region, and is proportional to wind speed (rough sea = more energy). Longer waves modulate shorter ones, and as wind speed increases, longer waves form.

Bragg scattering

Bragg resonance

Some waves are able to produce constructive interference, generating resonant (Bragg) waves:

λB = λ / (2 sin θ) λ and λB are of the same order of magnitude. Capillary waves, being centimetre-scale, are the ones that create Bragg scattering.

Gravitational waves, on the other hand, influence sea backscattering only indirectly, by modulating the Bragg backscattering (tilting the surface that the capillary waves sit on).

Polarization facts to quote
  • In pure Bragg scattering there is no cross-polarized return: VH = HV = 0.
  • There is a complete correlation between the two co-polarized channels.
  • The reflection coefficient α is ∝ permittivity ε and ∝ frequency f.
  • Backscattering is polarization dependent and decreases with increasing angle of incidence.
  • In general there is more backscattering, at the same incidence angle, with VV polarization — VV detects vertical targets (buildings, vegetation stalks) better.
  • For the sea: |αVV| > |αHH| ⇒ σ°VV > σ°HH.

Application: the wind field

The core problem

Wind estimation is an underdetermined (inverse) problem — there are ambiguous solutions. Consequently at least three measurements are needed. (In a noise-free environment, 2 independent measurements would be enough to make the problem solvable; in reality you want 3+.)

  • The NRCS that has the highest sensitivity to wind speed occurs at moderate incidence angles: θ = 30°–60°.
  • Therefore an off-nadir radar configuration is preferred → side-looking radar.
  • Wind measurements are indirect estimates given by the difference in backscattered energy: rough seas reflect a stronger signal than calm seas.
  • An ambiguity about wind direction remains, because of the sinusoidal relationship between azimuth and backscattering.

The wind field is a 2D vector function characterised by intensity and direction, generated by models inferred from joint radar and in-situ measurements with different sampling rates. The curves of the Geophysical Model Function (GMF) represent the set of wind intensity and direction values consistent with a measured σ°.

Observation

Over land it is not possible to obtain the wind field, because Bragg backscattering is absent — there are no capillary waves to resonate with.

Statement

The Rayleigh criterion here indicates the ability of the spectrometer (or telescope) to distinguish two spectral lines close together. When light passes through a slit, a sinc pattern is created. Each point inside the slit is a source and creates a wavefront. The total travelling plane wave is the sum of the interferences of all the spherical wavefronts. 14 RADAR and SAR

Two independent resolutions — range and azimuth — solved by two completely different tricks: the chirp and the synthetic aperture.

Definition

Radar is an active microwave sensor that measures target backscatter by delaying the signal in time after it is initially generated. A beam of successive pulses is transmitted at regular intervals (the PRF), and the surface is illuminated obliquely. SLAR = Side-Looking Airborne Radar.

Why side-looking and never nadir

A radar measures time of flight. Looking straight down, the left and right sides of the swath are equidistant from the antenna, so their echoes arrive simultaneously and become indistinguishable. Tilting the beam makes range a monotonic function of ground position.

Range resolution

Two adjacent objects have different echoes. If the backscattered portion of the second object overlaps with the first, they are detected as a single target.

ρr = cτ / 2 ≈ c / 2B slant-range resolution. τ = pulse duration, B = bandwidth. Ground range: ρg = ρr / sin θ.
γ = look angleθ = angle of incidence

To improve resolution, pulses should be as short as possible. But a short pulse carries little energy → poor SNR. That contradiction is what the chirp resolves.

Azimuth resolution and the antenna diffraction limit

Antenna diffraction limit: as the beamwidth decreases, the azimuthal resolution increases. A wider aperture produces a narrower beam, and therefore finer resolution.

Two targets can be detected separately if the distance between them is greater than the length of the radar beam W:

W = λ / L   (beamwidth)   →   Δx = R · W = Rλ / L R = slant range, L = physical antenna length. This is the real-aperture azimuth resolution.
ΔxSAR = L / 2   with  d ≤ L/2 The synthetic-aperture azimuth resolution — astonishingly, it is independent of range and of wavelength, and a smaller antenna gives a finer resolution.
The central trade in one sentence

The spatial resolution in range is obtained with high-energy pulses (therefore very expensive), while the spatial resolution in azimuth would require very large antennas — which is why we build synthetic antennas instead: the satellite's own motion synthesises an aperture kilometres long.

The CHIRP and pulse compression

Chirp

A chirp is a long, linearly frequency-modulated pulse. Its energy is distributed over a longer duration (so the transmitter never needs an impossible peak power) and is then compressed at reception using a matched filter — a convolution integral that compares the received signal with a replica of the transmitted one. The image is processed in the Fourier domain.

What you receive is an attenuated and delayed chirp. The compressed signal is a sinc, whose width is 1/B. The Nyquist sampling theorem governs the reception sampling.

Time compression = large bandwidth = better range resolution Compression ratio = τ·B. A 30 µs chirp with B = 50 MHz behaves like a 20 ns pulse — with 1500× more energy.

15 Speckle

The grainy texture of every SAR image — not noise from the instrument, but physics from coherence.

What speckle is

Speckle is a granular effect present in radar (SAR) images. It occurs because the radar illuminates a terrain cell containing many small scatterers (rocks, vegetation, buildings…). The reflected waves add together coherently: their amplitudes combine and their random phases interfere. The result: the image appears spotted or grainy even if the terrain is homogeneous.

Therefore speckle is NOT instrumental noise, but an intrinsic phenomenon of coherent radarmetry. Two generic resolution cells will simply have a different distribution of scattering elements.

In the acquisition there are therefore fluctuations with spots — band-limited random oscillations that attenuate the signal in the pixel. The fluctuations affect both the intensity and the phase of the signal: if two contributions have opposite phases (180°), they cancel each other out.

16 PolSAR — Radar Polarimetry

Transmit on two polarizations, receive on two, and the 2×2 matrix you get back tells you what kind of object you are looking at.

PolSAR

PolSAR (polarimeter) measures the polarization of EM waves, transmitting vertically and horizontally (linear H/V basis). It transmits linearly (e.g. H) and receives both the co-polarized (H) and the cross-polarized (V) scattered waves.

S = [ SHH   SHV ;   SVH   SVV ] the scattering matrix. First index = received polarization, second = transmitted.
  • Reciprocity hypothesis: SHV = SVH (true for a monostatic radar) → only 3 independent complex numbers.
  • S is a 1st-order descriptor. To effectively extract scattering information, 2nd-order descriptors are needed (covariance C and coherency T matrices, from which decompositions like Pauli / Freeman–Durden / Cloude–Pottier are built).
The operational cost

The pulse is split in two (V and H) and the pulse repetition time (PRt) is doubled. Four measurements are taken, two for each transmitted polarization. This lets you view the same area in different ways, and more information is obtained through differentiation between the channels. The price: half the swath, or half the azimuth sampling.

Finally we differenced pre/post NRCS in dB for VV and VH; VH was more informative because it is less affected by humidity changes and more sensitive to volume scattering."

Twelve sentences that answer half the exam

  1. The exponential is preferred because its domain is less limited — it describes free space and lossy propagation, splitting into decay × phase.
  2. Penetration depth δ = 1/α; more attenuation ⇒ shallower reach ⇒ more energy converted into heat.
  3. Polarization = the locus of the tip of E in the plane orthogonal to propagation, in the time domain — always closed, generally an ellipse.
  4. Fully polarized ⇔ |µxy| = 1 ⇔ det(W) = 0 ⇔ P = 1.
  5. At the Brewster angle ΓTM = 0: only horizontal (TE) polarization is reflected. TE has no Brewster angle.
  6. Roughness is relative: ΔΦ = 4πσcosθ/λ. Larger λ or larger θ ⇒ the surface looks smoother.
  7. Reflection and emission are complementary: emit a lot ⇒ reflect little.
  8. A radiometer measures brightness temperature TB = e·T, never the physical temperature directly.
  9. Scattering regimes are set by L/λ: Rayleigh (∝λ⁻⁴), Mie (isotropic, λ-independent), optical (direction-dependent, f-independent).
  10. The scatterometer is active, non-imaging, real-aperture, side-looking at 30–60°, deliberately coarse — and needs ≥3 azimuth looks to remove wind-direction ambiguity.
  11. SAR: range resolution from bandwidth (chirp + matched filter → sinc), azimuth resolution from the synthetic aperture (Δx = L/2, independent of range and λ).
  12. Speckle is not noise: it is coherent interference of many scatterers — Rayleigh amplitude, uniform phase, multiplicative.

Fundamentals & polarization

Why do we use the exponential rather than the sinusoidal representation?
Because the exponential has a less limited domain: with a complex exponent γ = α + jβ it describes both the oscillation and the attenuation in one expression, so the same formula covers free-space and lossy propagation. It also factorises cleanly into e−αz (amplitude decay → heat) times e−jβz (pure phase rotation → travel).
What is the difference between a progressive and a regressive wave?
Progressive → e−x → propagation, the transmitted signal from source to ground. Regressive → e+x → interaction, the reflected signal from ground back to source. The direction of propagation is k̂, which lies on the plane of incidence.
Define penetration depth and explain what happens as α increases.
δ = 1/α, the depth at which the amplitude falls to 1/e. As α increases the wave's energy stops earlier — it reaches zero over a shorter distance, and the lost energy is transformed into heat. Less attenuation ⇒ the wave reaches deeper into the medium ⇒ fewer losses.
What characterises a medium? Conductor vs insulator.
Permittivity ε, permeability µ, conductivity σ. Conductor: σ ≠ 0 (ideally ∞) — there are losses, part of the EM energy becomes heat. Insulator: σ = 0 — no attenuation, a perfect dielectric.
Active vs reactive power — where does each matter?
Active power is the real part and it travels — this is what matters for telecommunications. Reactive power is the imaginary part and does not travel; it is stored in the near field, which is why it matters for antennas.
Define polarization rigorously.
The orientation of the oscillation of the electric field: formally the locus traced by the tip of E in the plane orthogonal to the direction of propagation, in the time domain. It is always a closed curve, and in the most general case an ellipse. Its shape depends on the amplitudes Ex, Ey and their phase difference δ.
When does it make sense to talk about polarization at all?
Only if there is a discontinuity — two media with different properties. Interaction with a discontinuity is what changes the polarization state, and hence what makes it carry information.
Why do we need the polarization (coherence) matrix?
Because partially polarized waves fluctuate randomly — there is no single deterministic ellipse to describe. W is built from the correlations between Ex and Ey: its trace is the total power, its off-diagonal terms give the correlation normalised by µxy.
State the conditions for unpolarized and fully polarized fields.
Unpolarized: µxy = 0 and Wxx = Wyy. Fully polarized: |µxy| = 1, which implies det(W) = 0 (rank-1 matrix ⇒ one component is a fixed complex multiple of the other ⇒ a perfectly defined ellipse). A monochromatic wave is necessarily fully polarized.
What is the degree of polarization, and what do P = 0, 1 and in-between mean?
P is the ratio of polarized intensity to total intensity, 0 ≤ P ≤ 1. P = 1: the ellipse is perfectly defined. P = 0: the ellipse is not well defined — too many fluctuations, varying at every instant. 0 < P < 1: there is a trend, the ellipse varies gradually.
Depolarization vs repolarization.
Depolarization decreases the degree of polarization P (typical of volume scattering). Repolarization changes only the orientation — V → H or vice versa — while P stays equal to 1.

Fresnel, roughness

List the four interaction mechanisms with an obstacle.
Reflection (obstacles larger than λ), diffraction (pointed obstacles), refraction (transmitted through an interface), scattering (dispersion from a rough surface).
Why does the downlink suffer fewer losses than the uplink?
Path loss L = Pt/Pr increases with both distance and frequency, and Pr ∝ 1/R². The downlink is allocated the lower frequency, so it has lower loss — sensible, because the satellite has the tighter power budget.
Why can we only transmit in certain frequency bands?
Because the atmosphere absorbs certain bands (water vapour, oxygen, IR molecular bands). What remains usable are the atmospheric windows.
Describe the behaviour of ΓTM and ΓTE versus incidence angle.
ΓTM increases with angle and passes through zero at the Brewster angle — no reflection for that polarization. ΓTE decreases, becoming more negative, indicating a greater phase reversal on reflection. At 0° the two coincide (ΓTM = ΓTE); at 90°, grazing, everything is reflected.
What is special about the Brewster angle for remote sensing?
At θB = arctan(n₂/n₁), ΓTM = 0: the vertically polarized energy is completely transmitted into the second medium, so only the horizontal polarization (TE) can be measured in reflection. TE has no Brewster angle — its reflection never cancels.
What do ρ = +1 and ρ = −1 mean?
ρ = +1: the wave is completely reflected without phase inversion. ρ = −1: completely reflected but with a 180° phase inversion.
Give the Rayleigh roughness criterion and explain what "apparent" means.
ΔΦ = 4πσcosθi/λ, with the surface elevation assumed Gaussian, zero mean, standard deviation σ. Roughness is "apparent" because it depends on the wavelength and the angle of incidence, not on the surface alone: increasing θi means less roughness perceived; increasing λ makes the surface appear smoother. All the surfaces in the classic figure are physically rough, yet the first is seen as smooth by that wave.
What is the penalty factor f(σs)?
A factor given by the roughness that reduces the specularly reflected component. f(σs) = 1 for a completely smooth surface (ΔΦ = 0, at grazing θi = 90°); f(σs) = 0 for a rough surface (ΔΦ ≠ 0). As roughness grows, energy is scattered more and the reflected contribution falls.

Black body, radiometry, descriptors

Define a black body and a gray body.
A black body absorbs completely without reflecting (ideally opaque) and generates thermal radiation proportional to its temperature — an ideal absorber and emitter. A gray body is a narrow-band approximation of a real body: emissivity is constant over given spectral regions, so e does not depend on frequency there.
State Planck, Stefan–Boltzmann and Wien, and their two series approximations.
Planck: B depends on f, c, h, k, T — full spectral shape. Stefan–Boltzmann: B ∝ T⁴ — total power. Wien displacement: the peak shifts left, to shorter λ, as T increases (found from dBf/df = 0). Approximations: Wien for high frequencies → infrared; Rayleigh–Jeans for low frequencies → microwaves (B linear in T).
Why is reflection complementary to emission?
Kirchhoff: at equilibrium, emissivity equals absorptivity; for an opaque body absorptivity = 1 − reflectivity. So a body that emits strongly at a frequency reflects weakly there, and vice versa.
What is brightness temperature and why is it always ≤ T?
TB is the temperature a black body would need to radiate like the real body: e(θ,φ,p,λ,T) = TB/T, so TB = eT. Since 0 ≤ e ≤ 1, TB ≤ T. A radiometer measures TB; recovering T requires knowing e.
Coherent vs incoherent light — and the EO consequence.
Coherent: waves harmonically dependent, correlated, fixed phase relation (laser, SAR) — phase is usable, which enables interferometry but produces speckle. Incoherent: waves independent, no phase correlation, hence non-polarized (natural emission) — only intensity is usable, no speckle.
Radiance vs brightness vs irradiance.
Irradiance E [W/m²] is incident power density; exitance M is leaving power density; radiance L and brightness B are the same physical quantity (power per area per solid angle) — "radiance" in optics for reception, "brightness/brilliance" in microwave for emission. E = πB. Power is the integral over solid angle and frequency, which is why B is frequency-dependent.
What is a Lambertian surface, and what is its opposite?
One whose spectral radiance is constant with respect to all angles — the ideal rough diffuser; for it, ρ = w (independent of direction and polarization). Its opposite is the perfectly smooth Fresnel surface, whose reflection exists in a single direction and is described by two Dirac deltas: Ωs = δ(θs−θi)·δ(φs−φsp).
Write the energy balance descriptors.
αf = dPa/dPi (absorbance → heat → loss), tf = dPt/dPi (transmittance), wf = dPs/dPi = Mf/Ef (albedo/reflectivity). Conservation: dPi = dPa+dPt+dPs ⇒ αf+tf+wf = 1.
Why must the RCS be normalized for a distributed target?
Because σ scales with the illuminated area, which depends on the geometry and the sensor, not on the terrain. Dividing by A gives σ° = dσ/dA — dimensionless, comparable between sensors, and a genuine property of the surface. Since the surface is random, one also averages the coherent and incoherent contributions.
How are σ° and ρ related?
Through the BRDF: σ°qp = 4ρqpcosθscosθi. σ is the radar language, ρ the optical one.

Radiative transfer & scattering

SRT vs VRT.
Scalar Radiative Transfer is independent of polarization and used at optical frequencies. Vector Radiative Transfer depends on polarization and is used at microwave frequencies (Stokes vector formulation).
Write and explain the formal solution of the RT equation.
B(r) = B(0)e−τ(0,r) + ∫₀r ke(r′)J(r′)e−τ(r′,r)dr′. The brilliance from the origin is attenuated by the medium's extinction; at every point r′ there is a source emitting and scattering contributions keJ, which add up over each infinitesimal dr′ and are themselves attenuated by the optical thickness from r′ to r. The integral contains both scattering and absorption contributions, so terms can be positive or negative.
Define optical thickness and single-scattering albedo.
dτ = kedr with ke = ka + ks; w = ks/ke. The total source function is J = (1−w)Ja + wJs, and the equation reads dB/dτ + B = J.
Give the three scattering regimes with their λ dependence.
Rayleigh, L < λ: scattering ∝ λ⁻⁴. Mie, L ~ λ: uniform in all directions, independent of λ. Optical, L > λ: depends on direction but not on frequency. Volumetric scattering happens when L/λ ~ 1; if L/λ ≪ 1 there is essentially none.
Raman vs fluorescence.
Raman: photons decay to a lower level with emission of photons at high frequencies — an inelastic shift carrying a molecular fingerprint. Fluorescence: emission of photons at lower frequencies than the excitation. Both are inelastic, unlike Rayleigh/Mie/optical scattering.
What makes up the apparent brightness at the sensor?
Incident radiation, plus radiation from the ground, plus radiation from the atmosphere both upwelling and downwelling. In VIS/NIR, reflectivity dominates (and clouds disperse the signal); in mid/thermal IR and microwave, emission dominates and a passive system is used.

Scatterometer

Define a scatterometer in one sentence.
An active, non-imaging, real-aperture microwave radar that measures backscatter to retrieve geophysical properties — wide coverage, day/night, all-weather, typically on a near-polar sun-synchronous orbit.
Explain Bragg scattering over the sea.
Certain sea waves produce constructive interference — resonant Bragg waves — when λB = λ/(2 sinθ), with λ and λB of the same order. Centimetre-scale capillary waves, driven by the local wind, create the Bragg scattering; metre-scale gravitational waves only act indirectly, by modulating (tilting) the Bragg backscatter. Pure Bragg gives VH = HV = 0 and complete correlation between the co-pol channels.
Why is wind retrieval ambiguous, and how is it solved?
The relationship between backscatter and azimuth is sinusoidal, so several (speed, direction) pairs match a single σ° — the inverse problem is underdetermined and nonlinear. Solution: at least three measurements at different azimuth angles (two would suffice only in a noise-free case). The GMF curves give candidate solutions, the minima are sought, and the ambiguous intersections are removed by dealiasing.
Why must a scatterometer have coarse resolution and look sideways?
Sideways at θ = 30°–60° because that is where the NRCS is most sensitive to wind speed. Coarse resolution because the measurement must capture the short capillary waves without being contaminated by the long gravity waves — the cell must be big enough that the long-wave tilt averages out (resolution plane taken tangent to the long-wave curve).
Compare fan beam and pencil beam.
Fan beam: long dwell time, high SNR, fixed azimuth so data recovery is easy, large area covered. Pencil beam: conical scan, no nadir gap. SeaWinds uses two helical beams; the rotating fan beam is a slowly rotating fan combining both benefits. A "hole" in the coverage means no information; dwell time is the observation time, and longer dwell means greater accuracy.

Optical instruments

Spectrometer vs radiometer.
A spectrometer separates the intensities by wavelength — fine spectral resolution. A radiometer integrates all intensities in the band — one value per band. Both are passive; the active optical instrument is LIDAR.
List the wavelength-separation methods.
Prism (dispersion); grating spectrometer (sinθv = mλ/d — separation by angle, rays in resonance/in phase, a Bragg-like phenomenon); radiometer with filter wheels; Fabry–Pérot interferometer (multiple reflections between two partially silvered surfaces creating staggered, interfering beams).
State the Rayleigh resolution criterion and the diffraction limit.
Passing through a slit produces a sinc. Two sources are separable only if the peak of the second falls on the first minimum of the first; otherwise one lies within 3 dB of the other and they merge. The diffraction limit is θ > 1.22λ/D. By Huygens, each point in the slit is a source and the total wave is the sum of all spherical wavefronts; multiple slits increase the resolution.
Define FOV, IFOV and swath.
FOV is the maximum observation area; IFOV applies to one pixel. Swath = 2H·tan(FOV/2) = (L/f)·H, with L = Nd·d the length of the detector array. As the viewing angle increases, resolution degrades.
Whisk broom vs push broom.
Whisk broom scans across track with a moving mirror, detecting multiple parallel lines at once, with high detection/integration time and filters splitting the radiation into band-specific signals — giving a 3D image (width × height × band). Push broom scans along track: the mirror is fixed and there are many detectors, a linear array of lens-based detectors each measuring one resolution cell, giving a swath greater than whisk broom.

SAR, speckle, InSAR

Why is radar side-looking?
Because it measures time of flight. At nadir, symmetric points left and right are equidistant and their echoes arrive together, so they cannot be separated. An oblique geometry makes range monotonic across the swath. SLAR = Side-Looking Airborne Radar.
Give range and azimuth resolutions and their trade-offs.
Slant range: ρr = cτ/2 ≈ c/2B — improved by shorter pulses, i.e. wider bandwidth, which demands high-energy (expensive) pulses. Real-aperture azimuth: W = λ/L and Δx = Rλ/L — needs a very large antenna. Synthetic aperture: Δx = L/2, so a shorter antenna gives finer resolution, independent of range and wavelength.
Explain the chirp and pulse compression.
A chirp is a long, linearly frequency-modulated pulse whose energy is spread over a long duration, then compressed at reception by a matched filter — a convolution of the received signal with a replica of the transmitted one, done in the Fourier domain. What returns is an attenuated, delayed chirp; the compressed output is a sinc of width 1/B. Nyquist governs the sampling. Time compression ⇒ large bandwidth ⇒ better range resolution, without needing an impossible peak power.
Compare Spotlight, StripMap, ScanSAR and TOPSAR.
Spotlight: beam steered to stare at one target — best resolution, tiny coverage. StripMap: fixed beam, continuous strip. ScanSAR: hops between elevation angles for multiple sub-swaths — wide coverage but low resolution. TOPSAR: a mosaic of sub-swaths with azimuth steering, giving uniform quality — used by Sentinel-1 IW.
Is speckle noise? Explain its origin and statistics.
No — it is an intrinsic phenomenon of coherent radarmetry. Each resolution cell contains many small scatterers whose reflected waves add coherently: amplitudes combine and random phases interfere, so a homogeneous terrain still looks grainy. I and Q are each sums of many independent variables ⇒ Gaussian(0,σ²) by the Central Limit Theorem ⇒ amplitude r = √(I²+Q²) is Rayleigh-distributed, and the phase is uniform on [0,2π) because no angle is privileged. Speckle is multiplicative: it scales the signal proportionally.
How do you reduce speckle, and at what cost?
Multi-looking (average nearby pixels): reduces variance but loses spatial resolution. Adaptive filters (Lee, Frost, Gamma MAP): smooth while preserving edges by adapting to local statistics. Polarimetry: uses HH/HV/VV to separate signal from speckle. Multitemporal stacking: averages many acquisitions — key for InSAR and radiometric stability.
What is the scattering matrix, and why are 2nd-order descriptors needed?
S = [SHH SHV; SVH SVV], with reciprocity SHV = SVH for a monostatic radar. S is a 1st-order descriptor — a single complex realisation, contaminated by speckle. To extract stable scattering information you need 2nd-order descriptors (covariance/coherency matrices), which involve averaging and support decompositions into surface, double-bounce and volume mechanisms. Operationally, polarimetry splits the pulse into V and H and doubles the pulse repetition time, giving four measurements.
What does an interferogram show, and what is one fringe worth?
The point-by-point phase shift between pre- and post-event signals, wrapped in 2π. A negative shift means the target moved away (longer travel time); positive means it moved closer. One full colour cycle = one fringe = λ/2 of LOS displacement — 2.8 cm for Sentinel-1 C band (λ = 5.6 cm). Reading follows the orbit direction; ascending, red→red means uplift and blue→blue means subsidence.
How do you interpret fringe shape?
Concentrated and concentric around a point → the epicentre of a strong earthquake, with violent deformation and evident surface breaks. Spaced, thinned and irregular → plate slippage producing a deep rupture; the wave diffused and lost energy — typical of a medium-magnitude event such as M 5.1.
What is coherence and what destroys it?
The correlation between pre- and post-event signals — how well each point matches itself later. High when scattering is stable (same scatterers before and after); low when scattering changes: obvious terrain changes, vegetation, water, or terrain with different dielectric properties such as snow or rain. Low coherence means the interferometric phase there is meaningless.
Why C band rather than X band for this interferogram?
In C band the wavelength is comparable to the size of the observed targets, giving good coherence. X band has a shorter wavelength, so it would give finer resolution but much more incoherence — small scene changes are already a large fraction of λ and destroy the phase.
VV or VH — which would you use, and why?
VV is more sensitive to atmospheric effects that change the surface dielectric properties (humidity, heavy rain), so only in optimal conditions does its very strong backscatter make it better. VH is less sensitive to those effects and has weaker backscatter in good conditions, but it is more useful in hurricanes or heavy rain, where it measures the component depolarized by water droplets. VH is dominated by volume scattering, so it is more tied to the composition of the target — good for forests and landslides. Neither depends on cloud cover, because it is SAR.
Walk me through your SNAP processing chain and justify each step.
Split (one swath, four bursts around the epicentre) to cut the data down; Interferogram Formation with Flat-Earth phase removal to eliminate the deterministic ellipsoidal ramp; Goldstein filtering to correct noise and phase oscillation so fringes are countable; topographic phase removal with a DEM so only event-driven deformation remains, not relief; Terrain Correction to georeference and align. Then NRCS analysis in VV and VH, pre vs post, differenced on a logarithmic scale with multi-looking and a threshold.
